## 答案与解析

**1.** A. It divides GDP by population, so countries of different size can be compared  
　Explanation: Per capita GDP equals GDP divided by population, addressing the issue of large countries having naturally high totals, but it still doesn't reflect the fairness of distribution and can't replace indicators like HDI.  
　出处：Term 1 · Week 2 Global — Measuring and Mapping Human Wellbeing

**2.** B. Health, education and standard of living (GNI per capita)  
　Explanation: The three dimensions of HDI are health, education, and standard of living, making it more comprehensive than single economic indicators like GDP as it includes health and education outside of GDP.  
　出处：Term 1 · Week 2 Global — Lesson 3: quantitative (GDP) vs qualitative (HDI)

**3.** C. A smaller workforce supporting a larger elderly group, straining pensions  
　Explanation: An elderly population of nearly 40% means an increased dependency ratio, reduced workforce, and financial pressure on healthcare and aged care, whereas a 'population pyramid with a very wide base and a much narrower top' describes a high birth rate and expanding population, the opposite of an ageing population.  
　出处：Term 1 · Weeks 3–4 Geographic Skills — China's Ageing Population (37.8% over 60 by 2100)

**4.** Advantage is concentrated in the east, north and harbour-side suburbs, while disadvantage is concentrated in the west and south-west. Higher incomes, employment and better access to services, schools and healthy food cluster in the advantaged areas, while high housing costs push lower-income households further from those opportunities.  
　Explanation: The key points to answer are 'pattern plus one cause': pattern, which is the difference between the east/north and west/southwest, and cause, which can be income, housing prices, or accessibility of services and employment, as required by the course scaffold.  
　出处：Term 1 · Weeks 7–8 Local — Sydney's socio-economic divide (East vs West)

**5.** D. A sentencing process where Elders sit with a magistrate after a guilty verdict  
　Explanation: Circle sentencing occurs after a guilty verdict and involves community members and elders discussing sentencing to increase cultural adaptability and reduce reoffending, without replacing the court or being a pre-trial process.  
　出处：Term 2 · Week 2.3 First Nations Justice — Action (circle sentencing)

**6.** A. Three parts of a whole that add to 100%, read off one point  
　Explanation: A ternary graph shows the proportion of three components that add up to 100%, such as employment structure or soil composition, and should be read along each axis, with the three components always adding up to 100.  
　出处：Term 2 · Week 10.1 Ternary Graphs

**7.** B. An AR uses four figures for a square; a GR uses six for a point  
　Explanation: AR uses four digits to locate a square, approximately 1km x 1km, while GR uses six digits to locate a specific point within that square, accurate to 100m, and the order of the digits should not be confused.  
　出处：Term 1 · Weeks 3–4 Topographic Skills — AR, GR and elevation

**8.** It involves Elders and community members in deciding the sentence, so the outcome reflects the offender's community and culture and the offender is held accountable in front of people they know. This can increase respect for the process and reduce reoffending, which matters given the over-representation of Aboriginal people in custody.  
　Explanation: You need to write 'mechanism → result': community and elder involvement leads to cultural safety and accountability, making it more likely to be accepted and reducing reoffending rates, and just writing 'fairer' won't get you any marks.  
　出处：Term 2 · Weeks 2.3 / 3.1 First Nations Justice — Action & Outcome

**9.** C. Selecting data that support a position while ignoring contrary data  
　Explanation: Cherry-picking is one of the five FLICC techniques, where only favourable data points are selected and the overall trend is ignored, which is what the reflection questions in class are trying to get you to think about.  
　出处：Term 4 · Lessons 1.1–1.2 Climate misinformation (FLICC) / Lesson 2.1

**10.** D. Human greenhouse gas emissions trap more infrared radiation, adding to warming  
　Explanation: The natural greenhouse effect makes the Earth habitable, while human emissions of extra greenhouse gases enhance it, absorbing and re-radiating more infrared radiation back to the surface, and distinguishing between the two is a common exam point.  
　出处：Term 4 · Lesson 2.2 What are the causes of climate change? (Part 1)

**11.** A. Heat stress makes corals expel their symbiotic algae, losing colour and food  
　Explanation: Corals have a symbiotic relationship with zooxanthellae; when stressed by high temperatures, corals expel the zooxanthellae, losing their colour and primary source of nutrition, and if the high temperatures persist, the corals will die, not just turn white.  
　出处：Term 4 · Lesson 3.3 How does sea temperature rise lead to coral bleaching?

**12.** B. Global warming is rising average temperature; climate change also covers rainfall and sea level  
　Explanation: Global warming refers to the rise in average temperature, which is just one part of climate change, a broader concept that also includes changes in precipitation, sea levels, ice caps, and extreme weather events.  
　出处：Term 4 · Lesson 2.1 EVIDENCE — distinguishing global warming from climate change

**13.** The ocean absorbs extra carbon dioxide, which forms carbonic acid and lowers the pH of seawater. This reduces the availability of carbonate ions, so organisms such as corals, shellfish and some plankton cannot build or maintain their shells and skeletons, which then affects the wider food chain.  
　Explanation: The mechanism involves: CO2 dissolving in seawater → forming carbonic acid → decreasing pH → reducing carbonate ions → making it difficult for calcifying organisms to form shells and skeletons → affecting the food chain.  
　出处：Term 4 · Lesson 4.1 Ocean Acidification & Its Impact on Marine Life

**14.** First, higher seas and larger storm surges erode beaches and inundate low-lying land, so villages, gardens and fresh-water supplies are lost and communities are forced to relocate. Second, salt water intrudes into soil and groundwater, reducing the land available for growing food and increasing dependence on outside aid.  
　Explanation: This can be answered on two levels: environmental and land (erosion, flooding, saltwater intrusion) and socio-economic (forced relocation, food and water shortages, reliance on aid).  
　出处：Term 4 · Lesson 3.2 Sea level rise — Solomon Islands case study
