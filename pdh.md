## Answers and explanations

**1.** Positive: trying a new sport such as rock climbing or martial arts; joining the school play or band; volunteering for a community cause or running for the school committee. Negative: binge drinking or vaping; sexting; getting a lift with a driver who has been drinking; dangerous driving; skipping school; trespassing or vandalism.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for three positive examples (1 mark each, 3 marks) and three negative examples (1 mark each, 3 marks). An extra mark is available for explaining the difference: positive risks carry uncertainty but bring new skills or growth, whereas negative risks bring harm, legal consequences or loss of health.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Risk taking

**2.** Barriers: cost; fear about confidentiality; low health literacy (not realising a condition is treatable); transport and distance, especially in rural areas; long waiting times and complicated booking systems; language and communication difficulties; stigma and embarrassment; not knowing which service to contact. Effects: (1) Cost — a young person delays seeing a GP or dentist, so a treatable condition worsens and may eventually need hospital care. (2) Fear about confidentiality — they avoid discussing mental or sexual health with a doctor, so the problem goes untreated and can escalate into a crisis.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for listing five barriers (1 mark each, 5 marks) and explaining the health effect of two of them (1 mark each, 2 marks). The explanation has to show a causal chain of 'delay → deterioration, complications or crisis'. One mark is for expression and structure.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Barriers to accessing health services

**3.** A general practitioner (GP) under Medicare; Headspace or another youth mental health service; the school counsellor; a dental clinic; a sexual health clinic; physiotherapy; a community health centre; a pharmacy; a hospital emergency department; a dietitian or nutritional support service.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for naming a specific service (1 mark each, 5 marks in total); a named institution or a type of service is required. An extra mark is available for noting which services are free or low-cost for adolescents (Medicare, Headspace, school counsellors).  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Health services for young people

**4.** A. Speeding — Fatigue — Drink/drug impairment — Not wearing a seatbelt — Distraction  
  Explanation: Marking points are broken down into correct ordering, with 2 points for a fully correct order and 1 point for only the first half correct, based on 2025 NSW data: speeding first (around 39–40% of fatal crashes involved speeding, 134 deaths), fatigue second (around 20–21%, 75 deaths), drink-driving third, not wearing a seatbelt fourth (29 deaths), and distraction fifth (often underreported).  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Rank the top 5 causes + Term 1 · Lesson 2–3 (2025 Fatal Five)

**5.** (a) Human factor. (b) Vehicle factor. (c) Environmental factor. The human category is easiest to change, because the driver directly controls their own behaviour — speed, phone use, seatbelt use, fatigue and alcohol can all be changed through education, laws and police enforcement. Vehicle and environmental factors also matter, but about 95% of crashes involve human factors alone or in combination, so changing driver behaviour has the biggest effect on the road toll.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for classifying all three categories (1 mark each, 3 marks). Identifying human factors as the most changeable is worth 1 mark, and the reasons are worth 2 marks: human factors are controllable, and around 95% of crashes involve human error, which can be changed through education, legislation and enforcement.  
  Source: Term 1 · Lesson 2–3 Factors influencing road and traffic incidents (matches the "list the major causes" question)

**6.** B. Using a mobile phone and interacting with passengers  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for recognising two items (1 mark each, 2 marks); the two main ones are phone use and passenger interaction. Using a phone raises crash risk about fourfold, and P-plate drivers carrying three or more passengers more than double their crash rate.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Driver distractions + Term 1 · Lesson 2–3 & Lesson 5 Safety Plans

**7.** C. 0.00 for learner, 0.00 for provisional, 0.05 for full  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for filling in three blanks (1 mark each): Learner 0.00, Provisional 0.00 and Full 0.05. The most common mistake is writing 0.05 for a P-plate; in NSW both P-plate and L-plate drivers must be at zero, and only a full licence has a 0.05 limit.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · BAC (values per NSW legal standard; syllabus supplement)

**8.** D. Social, pleasure (intermittent reward) and pain  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for filling in three blanks (1 mark each, 3 marks): Social (e.g. Farmville), Pleasure/intermittent reward (e.g. Call of Duty) and Pain (e.g. World of Warcraft). Only specific examples are accepted, not general terms such as 'fun' or 'addictive'.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Games – three motivational drives + Term 1 · Lesson 9 Online Safety

**9.** Mutual respect; trust; honesty; open communication; consent; equality (no one holds all the power); freedom to be yourself; support and encouragement.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for each characteristic (1 mark each, 5 marks in total). Course terminology is required (mutual respect, trust, consent, open communication, equality), and only specific descriptions are accepted — not general terms such as 'treats me well' or 'doesn't argue'.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Features of respectful relationships

**10.** Using influence to support and encourage the other person; making decisions together and giving them a voice; standing up for someone who is being bullied; mentoring a younger player and sharing responsibility; being honest about your own limits and asking for their input; using a leadership role to make sure everyone is included.  
  Explanation: Marking points are broken down into each specific example, worth 1 point, usually given 3–4 points, with the criterion being 'power is used to give the other person more choices, making the relationship more equal', and negative uses of power, such as making all decisions for the other person, are also considered.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Positive use of power

**11.** Coercion or threats; manipulation and guilt-tripping; peer pressure to do something risky; bullying or public humiliation; controlling who the other person sees or what they wear; withholding money or information; using physical size or status to intimidate; pressuring someone about consent.  
  Explanation: Marking points are broken down into each specific example, worth 1 point, usually given 3–4 points, with negative power uses having in common 'using status, strength, or information difference to take away the other person's choices', including not just physical force but also emotional manipulation and economic control.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Negative use of power

**12.** Passive: holding back your own needs and going along with others, often to avoid conflict. Aggressive: putting your own needs first and disregarding or violating the other person's rights (shouting, blaming, threats). Passive-aggressive: expressing anger indirectly — sulking, sarcasm, silent treatment or gossiping instead of saying it directly. Assertive: stating your needs clearly and calmly while respecting the other person's needs, using 'I' statements and staying open to negotiation.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for naming the four styles (1 mark each, 4 marks) and describing each one accurately (1 mark each, 4 marks). The key is the two dimensions of 'my needs vs their needs'.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Four communication styles (marking points compiled from course content)

**13.** A sense of self is how a person sees and values themselves — their self-esteem, self-worth and personal identity. It includes their beliefs about who they are, their values, their strengths and weaknesses, and how they think others see them. It is shaped by family, peers, culture, media, school and personal experience, and it keeps changing through adolescence.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for: the definition (how a person views themselves), 1–2 marks; the components (self-esteem, self-worth, identity, values), 1–2 marks; and the influencing factors (family, peers, media, culture, experiences), 1 mark.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Sense of self (marking points compiled from course content)

**14.** Stigma is a cluster of negative attitudes and beliefs that lead people to fear, reject, avoid and discriminate against those with a mental illness. It labels the person rather than the illness. For a young person it can cause shame and isolation, lead to bullying or being left out, and stop them telling anyone or seeking treatment, so the condition gets worse.  
  Explanation: The marking criteria are broken down into: definition must include 'negative labels → fear/exclusion/discrimination' 2 marks; and the impact on adolescents 2 marks (shame and isolation, bullying or exclusion, and not daring to seek help leading to worsening conditions).  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Stigma + Term 2 · Lesson 2 Myths, Misconceptions & Stigma

**15.** Depression: persistent low mood and loss of interest for weeks, with low confidence, changed appetite and sleep, tiredness and, at worst, suicidal thoughts. Anxiety: excessive worry with physical tension, difficulty concentrating, avoidance of situations and being easily startled. Bipolar disorder: mood swings between manic highs (elevated energy, little sleep, risky behaviour) and depressive lows. Schizophrenia: disturbance of thinking and perception, including delusions and hallucinations, which changes how the person interprets reality. (Eating disorders and PTSD are also acceptable answers.)  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for each disorder name (1 mark) plus a description of its typical symptoms (1 mark), so the four disorders together are worth 4–8 marks. Descriptions must include symptoms that are unique to each disorder.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Common mental health disorders + Term 2 · Lesson 1

**16.** Social changes: teenagers develop their own values and are influenced more by peers and media than by parents, so conflict over rules increases; they form a sexual identity and want more privacy; they seek independence and greater responsibility, and want to negotiate rather than be told what to do. Emotional changes: moods and feelings swing more widely, they become more sensitive to others' opinions and self-conscious about how they look, and they want to make their own decisions. As a result the relationship shifts from parent-controlled to more equal, with more negotiation and sometimes more conflict, and it usually settles again as independence is worked out.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for: at least two social changes with explanations (2–3 marks); at least two emotional changes with explanations (2–3 marks); and identifying the overall shift in the relationship from 'being controlled' to 'negotiation/equality', with an explanation of the outcome (1–2 marks).  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Adolescent relationships

**17.** Gives eye contact and open body language; puts the phone away and stops other tasks; does not interrupt; lets the speaker finish before replying; asks clarifying questions; paraphrases or summarises what was said; shows empathy and does not judge; keeps the conversation confidential.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for each characteristic (1 mark each, usually 4–5 marks), and the features must be observable behaviours: eye contact, not interrupting, repeating key points, putting the phone down.  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Features of a good listener

**18.** Description: trust is the confidence that the other person will not hurt or betray you; honesty means telling the truth even when it is difficult; freedom is each person's right to their own friends, interests and choices; consent means a clear, freely given yes to any physical or emotional intimacy; open communication means saying what you think and feel, and listening in return; mutual respect means treating the other person as an equal whose needs matter. Interaction: without honesty there can be no trust, therefore a relationship built on small lies gradually loses its security; without open communication consent cannot be given or checked, so respect breaks down and conflict or harm follows. Implications: a relationship missing trust and respect tends to become controlling or collapse, leading to emotional harm, isolation and, for young people, damage to self-esteem and mental health.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for: describing four behaviours (1 mark each, 4 marks); using connecting words such as 'therefore', 'as a result' or 'leading to' to explain the causal links between behaviours (2–3 marks); and discussing the consequences and impact on individuals, families and mental health (2–3 marks).  
  Source: Year 10 PDHPE Yearly Revision Questions 2023 · Analysing respectful relationships

**19.** A. Check the area is safe for you, bystanders and the casualty  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for the sequence (1 mark — D comes first) and the reason (1 mark — the rescuer's safety comes first). D stands for Danger, not Defibrillation.  
  Source: Term 3 · Lesson 5 First Aid · DANGER

**20.** B. 30 compressions : 2 breaths  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for the ratio 30:2 (1 mark), and for knowing that CPR can be stopped in only three situations: help arrives, the casualty recovers, or the rescuer is physically unable to continue.  
  Source: Term 3 · Lesson 5 First Aid · CPR

**21.** Danger: stop safely with hazard lights on, keep clear of moving traffic and any fuel spill, and ask bystanders to warn oncoming vehicles. Response: check whether the rider responds by speaking to them and squeezing their shoulders. Send for help: call 000 or direct a bystander to do it. Airway: open the airway and clear any foreign material. Breathing: look, listen and feel; if breathing is not normal, start CPR at 30:2. Circulation: control any major bleeding. Defibrillation: apply an AED if available and follow the voice prompts.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for: D, describing the danger at the scene and how to protect yourself (2 marks); R, checking for a response (1 mark); S, calling for help and specifying 000 or directing someone to do it (1 mark); the A-B-C sequence and the judgement involved (3 marks); D, getting the AED (1 mark); and naming two or more dangers at the scene — oncoming cars, fuel, power lines, broken glass (1–2 marks).  
  Source: Term 1 · First Aid – Road Trauma + Term 3 · Lesson 5 First Aid · DRSABCD

**22.** C. About 33 m  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for the working: 60 ÷ 3.6 = 16.7 m/s, then × 2 seconds ≈ 33 m (1 mark).  
  Source: Term 1 · Lesson 2–3 · Are you driving blind?

**23.** D. Four times as likely  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for the answer, four times (1 mark), and for giving reasons — unfamiliarity with road conditions, undeveloped hazard perception and a tendency to take risks — for a further 1 mark.  
  Source: Term 1 · Lesson 4 Influences on Road Safety · Young People on the Roads

**24.** About one in five (20%). The most common are depression, anxiety and substance use disorders. They often occur together: a person with an anxiety disorder may go on to develop depression, and a person with depression may misuse alcohol or other drugs to cope, so one condition can trigger or hide another.  
  Explanation: The marks are broken down as follows: 20% (1 in 5) is 1 mark; three types each worth 1 mark (3 marks); explaining comorbidities and mutual triggers is 2 marks. Writing numbers as 1 in 10 or 50% will result in a loss of marks; simply stating that the three diseases are common without explaining their relationship will not be awarded marks.  
  Source: Term 2 · Lesson 1 Common Mental Health Conditions · Prevalence

**25.** A. A strong sense of control over their own life  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for identifying the correct characteristic (1 mark) and for listing additional ones (a further 1 mark). The five characteristics are: control, strong problem-solving skills, solid social connections, seeing oneself as a 'survivor' rather than a 'victim', and being able to seek help. 'Never seeking help' and a 'victim mentality' are the opposite.  
  Source: Term 2 · Lesson 3 Characteristics of Resilient people

**26.** Stigma: seeing mental illness as weakness or as 'not masculine' makes young men hide their feelings and bottle them up, so problems get worse; it can be reduced through mental health education and campaigns that present help-seeking as normal. Low mental health literacy: many cannot recognise their own symptoms or do not know which service to contact, so they wait until crisis point; this can be reduced by teaching help-seeking skills in school and promoting services such as ReachOut, Beyond Blue and Headspace.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for each barrier (1 mark) plus an explanation of why it stops help-seeking (1 mark), 4 marks in total, and for the matching improvement measure for each barrier (1 mark each, 2 marks). Barriers and measures must be matched one to one: using 'strengthening education' to solve 'high costs' is an invalid pairing and earns nothing.  
  Source: Term 2 · Lesson 8 Seeking Help · Barriers to seeking help

**27.** B. Nutritional support  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for recognising 'nutrition support' as a health service (1 mark) and for listing additional categories (a further 1 mark). Lesson 1 lists nine categories: mental health, dental, substance abuse treatment, preventive care, physical and occupational therapy, nutrition support, pharmaceutical services, transportation, and prenatal care. A nutritionist or dietitian sits in the nutrition support category.  
  Source: Term 3 · Lesson 1 Health Services · Types of Health services

**28.** They are self-management skills because adults must regulate their own daily routine — no one else plans their meals or schedules their exercise — and these habits strongly shape long-term health, energy levels and disease risk. Example: planning meals around the core food groups (vegetables, fruit, grains, lean protein, dairy or alternatives) instead of relying on takeaway; example: scheduling at least 60 minutes of moderate physical activity each day.  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for explaining why it is self-management — you have to plan it yourself, with no one planning it for you, and it directly affects long-term health (2 marks); a nutrition example (1 mark); a physical activity example (1 mark); and pointing out the link to disease risk (1 mark). Examples must name a specific behaviour; 'eating healthily' does not count.  
  Source: Term 3 · Lesson 8 Life Challenges · Self-Management Skills (nutrition and fitness detail supplemented from the syllabus)

**29.** C. What qualifications and training does the practitioner have?  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for choosing 'qualifications and training' (1 mark), and for supplementing that judgement with further questions drawn from the material (a further 2 marks): what treatment is provided, what effects it has, the cost, and whether it can be used alongside conventional medicine. A website's appearance, how often it has been forwarded and its price cannot prove effectiveness — this is a common trap in this type of question.  
  Source: Term 3 · Lesson 3 Assessing Credibility of Health Information

**30.** D. Advertising, friends, easy access and gambling-like games  
  Explanation: Marks are awarded for each of the four items (1 mark each, 4 marks): advertising, whether friends gamble (peer pressure), ease of access, and games that look like gambling. 'Cost, transport, language barriers and long waiting times' are barriers to health services, and 'personality, fatigue, alcohol use and speeding' are the Fatal Five of road safety, which students often confuse with this question.  
  Source: Term 1 · Lesson 8 Gambling · Potential Influences on Gambling (explicitly required by Study Guide Section 2)
