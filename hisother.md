## Answers and explanations

**1.** A. Explain how the perspective appears in the source and why it is there  
  Explanation: The material sets out three steps: first, identify the perspective; then explain how that perspective is presented in the source and why; simply summarising content without analysing perspective is a common way to lose marks.  
  Source: Term 1 · Practice Questions.pdf — Account for the perspective of Source A

**2.** B. Mobile killing squads  
  Explanation: The lesson is titled 'The Einsatzgruppen — mobile killing squads'. They followed the German army into Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union and killed on a large scale through mass shootings, a different form of killing from the concentration camp system.  
  Source: Term 1 · Lessons 3.2–4.1 The Einsatzgruppen (module list)

**3.** C. It shows the regime's dehumanising portrayal, not how Jews lived  
  Explanation: The value of a propaganda poster lies in what it reveals about the intentions of its creators and the stereotypes they promoted, not in the facts it states. Choosing 'no value' is the typical mistake: bias is itself important evidence about Nazi ideology.  
  Source: Term 1 · Lesson 1.3 Nazi propaganda & the skill of perspective

**4.** It reveals the perpetrator's perspective, language and motives — what the regime wanted its audience to believe and how it justified the attacks. The bias is itself evidence of Nazi intent, but the source must be checked against other evidence, such as victim testimony.  
  Explanation: Bias does not mean a source has no value: the perpetrator's perspective can reveal propaganda intentions and motives, but it must be used alongside other evidence such as victim testimony. Answers should attend to 'who and why', not simply to true or false.  
  Source: Term 1 · Practice Questions.pdf / Lesson 4.1 Propaganda value source analysis

**5.** Content is what the source says or shows — the events, people and details in it. Perspective is the point of view from which it was made, shaped by the author's side, purpose and audience. A historian needs the content as evidence and the perspective to judge how far that evidence can be trusted.  
  Explanation: Content answers 'what was said'; perspective answers 'who said it, from which side, and why'. Keeping the two apart lets you both use the source and evaluate it.  
  Source: Term 1 · Source Analysis Study Notes / Practice Questions

**6.** D. The Potsdam Conference  
  Explanation: The lesson sets out to understand the Potsdam Conference and its role in creating the Cold War conflict. The Yalta Conference also took place near the end of the war, but the course starts with Potsdam.  
  Source: Term 2 · Lesson 2.1 Origins of the Cold War (Potsdam Conference)

**7.** A. Marshall Plan — economic aid; NATO — collective security alliance  
  Explanation: The Marshall Plan used economic aid to rebuild Western Europe and reduce the appeal of communism, while NATO (1949) was a collective security alliance. Together they formed the containment system in Europe.  
  Source: Term 2 · Lesson 2.3 The Policy of Containment in Europe — Marshall Plan & NATO

**8.** B. The building of the Berlin Wall, dividing East from West Berlin  
  Explanation: In the 1961 Berlin Crisis, East Germany built the Berlin Wall with Soviet support to stop the outflow of its population, and the wall became a symbol of the Cold War division of Europe.  
  Source: Term 2 · Lesson 5.2 The Berlin Crisis (1961)

**9.** Truman relied mainly on economic and military aid (the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan) and on collective security through NATO, plus a limited conventional war in Korea. Eisenhower preferred nuclear deterrence, brinkmanship and covert CIA action because it contained communism at a lower cost.  
  Explanation: The difference lies in means and cost: Truman relied on economic aid and alliances, while Eisenhower relied on nuclear deterrence and covert operations. Answers also need the specific policy names.  
  Source: Term 2 · Unit essay question (Lessons 3.2 / 6.1 modelling)

**10.** The discovery of Soviet nuclear missiles in Cuba led to a US naval quarantine and a direct superpower standoff in which any miscalculation could have triggered nuclear war. It ended with the Soviet withdrawal of the missiles and a US pledge not to invade Cuba, and it pushed both sides towards arms control.  
  Explanation: The danger was direct confrontation between two nuclear powers with very little decision time. The outcome was the Soviet withdrawal of the missiles, a US promise not to invade Cuba (and a secret US withdrawal of missiles from Turkey), and a move towards arms-control talks.  
  Source: Term 2 · Lesson 5.3 JFK and the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)

**11.** C. A 1948 UN declaration of fundamental rights that influenced later conventions  
  Explanation: The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the UN General Assembly in 1948, is not a treaty. It provides the normative basis for later human rights conventions and for domestic law, and that is where its significance lies.  
  Source: Term 3 · Lesson 1.2 The significance of the UDHR to human rights & freedoms

**12.** D. Charles Perkins  
  Explanation: The material states clearly that Charles Perkins led the 1965 Freedom Ride. The other figures pair with the 1966 Wave Hill strike (Lingiari), the 1992 Mabo case and the 1938 Day of Mourning (Cooper).  
  Source: Term 3 · Civil Rights Comic Book Assessment Guide — Significant Individuals

**13.** A. It overturned terra nullius and recognised native title in law  
  Explanation: The Mabo decision rejected the legal doctrine of 'terra nullius' and recognised the land rights of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, which was a turning point in the land rights movement.  
  Source: Term 3 · Assessment Guide — Significant Individuals (Eddie Koiki Mabo)

**14.** They are the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children who were forcibly removed from their families by government agencies and church missions under protection and welfare laws, mainly across the twentieth century. The removals broke families and caused long-term trauma that still affects communities.  
  Explanation: The key point is forced removal rather than voluntary departure, carried out under state protection and welfare laws, with family disruption and intergenerational trauma as the result.  
  Source: Term 3 · Lesson 5.2 The Stolen Generations

**15.** Land rights are grants or recognition of land by government legislation — land returned or given to Aboriginal communities. Native title is a right recognised by the courts as belonging to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples whose traditional laws and customs connect them to the land; it is understood to have survived colonisation rather than being granted.  
  Explanation: The crucial difference lies in the source: land rights come from legislation and government grants, while native title comes from court-recognised traditional rights existing before colonisation (the Mabo decision).  
  Source: Term 3 · Lesson 5.3 The Significance of the Land Rights Movement & Native Title
